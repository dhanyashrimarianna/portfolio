Olist Growth & Retention Analytics Featured
PostgreSQLPythonstatsmodelsGit

The problem

A simulated business analyst engagement on a retention problem, using public Brazilian e-commerce data (99K orders). The project runs from requirements to recommendations.

What I did

  • Wrote a BRD with 9 KPIs and 13 requirements, 12 user stories with acceptance criteria, and a RACI stakeholder map.
  • Built a PostgreSQL star schema with 11 automated data-quality checks. It caught a customer-ID error that would have shown a false 0% repeat-purchase rate.
  • Ran funnel, cohort, and delivery analysis on 93K customers.
  • Ran a two-proportion z-test linking late delivery to lower repurchase, and checked it across product categories.
  • Prioritized four improvement opportunities with RICE and sensitivity analysis, drafted a PRD, and designed an A/B test (about 9,100 customers per group) to detect a +0.5-point lift in 90-day repeat rate.

What I found

  • Only 3.0% of customers placed a repeat order.
  • Late deliveries averaged 2.27 review stars, versus 4.29 for on-time ones (a 2.02-star gap).
  • Late first deliveries were linked to lower repurchase: 0.92% versus 1.25% (p = 0.027). Rates across categories ranged from 0.80% to 1.75%.

Coworking Market Analysis & Churn Benchmark PythonJupyterPower BILogistic Regression

The problem

A case study on the factors behind customer acquisition and churn in the coworking industry, such as pricing, amenities, and location. All of the data is simulated, and provider names are labels, not claims about real businesses.

What I did

  • Worked with a simulated dataset of 20,000 customers, about 268K usage rows, and 4,979 surveys.
  • Validated the data before analyzing it: I set aside one incomplete usage record and flagged 106 unusually large team sizes instead of quietly changing them.
  • Wrote the analysis in Python and a Jupyter notebook, and included the Power BI files in the repo.
  • Fit a logistic regression baseline to predict churn from company, location, monthly spend, and team size. I used a stratified 75/25 split and tested on 5,000 held-out customers.

What I found

  • The recorded churn share was 19.6% (3,913 of 20,000 customers).
  • The model reached an ROC AUC of 0.676, compared with 0.5 for a majority-class baseline. But it flagged none of the churned customers at the default threshold, and its 80% accuracy is about what always guessing “no churn” would score.
  • So I treat it as a baseline, not a prediction tool. This data can’t show that pricing or amenities cause churn, or reveal switching patterns.
